"WTF Ubuntu?": thread analysis against Canonical's enterprise pillars

Sources: chalmovsky.com/posts/wtf-ubuntu (published 2026-11-02) · Hacker News thread #50337608 · I read all 686 comments (snapshot taken 2026-11-12) · Patch status re-verified 2026-11-12

The original post, in brief

  • CVE-2026-98432 is a critical (CVSS 9.8) use-after-free in the Linux kernel's IPv4/TCP code. It has existed since 2016 and can be triggered remotely. Anthropic says it built a working root exploit.
  • Debian patched it on Oct 16. Two weeks later, every supported Ubuntu release, LTS included, was still listed as "Vulnerable".
  • The author contrasts that with Canonical's own marketing ("timely patches for critical vulnerabilities", "Enterprise-grade", "Build with confidence") and calls the wording empty.
  • The author took every Ubuntu server offline and is migrating away. They had picked Ubuntu as the "solid, no-drama LTS distro" and because it's the default image at most VPS providers.
  • Closing jab: volunteer-run distros (Debian, Arch, Alpine) shipped the fix. Canonical, which sells security subscriptions, did not.

What the thread concluded

  • Paying didn't help. Several people confirmed that Ubuntu Pro / ESM / Livepatch customers did not get the fix earlier. This undercut the "free users get free-tier service" defence.
  • Communication failed more badly than engineering. The fixed kernels were already in -proposed, but Ubuntu's advisory had no mitigations, no explanation of the priority, and text pasted from upstream.
  • Canonical's moat held up: 10–15 year LTS, FIPS/certifications, and vendor ecosystems (NVIDIA, CUDA, SQL Server, ROS, Jetson) are the main reasons people stay.
  • Debian benefited most (193 mentions). RHEL-family distros (Rocky, Alma) were the second choice, mainly for compliance-driven shops.

What changed since Nov 2

  • Resolved: standard-support LTS kernels (22.04, 24.04, 26.04, 26.10) shipped through the normal SRU path, with no out-of-cycle release.
  • Resolved: critical priority and a written mitigation, the two things the thread asked for most.
  • Not resolved: ESM (Pro), FIPS, and HWE kernels. That includes the 24.04 HWE 7.0 kernel, which people rely on for newer hardware.
  • Context: Canonical is about 9 days behind RHEL's main streams and 25 days behind Debian. The gap to RHEL, the closest enterprise peer, is the one that matters for enterprise buyers.

Pillar-by-pillar breakdown

Support & SLAs

Epicentre

Responsive support systems and clearly defined SLAs, so downtime is minimal and issues are resolved quickly.

Criticism

  • The core charge: "timely patches for critical vulnerabilities" was not delivered. Fixes took 2–3 weeks while volunteer distros needed hours to days.
  • Ubuntu Pro subscribers got nothing earlier. Several users with Pro servers confirmed this. "Apparently they don't owe anything to Ubuntu Pro subscribers too."
  • No usable SLA for CVE response. One commenter asked for an explicit "SLA" concept so an "I've got you covered" promise can be held to account.
  • Canonical's Sep 23 blog post, "Accelerating delivery of CVE fixes", was quoted as failing "every single listed requirement".
  • Canonical's reply that the "2-week SRU cycle is the right balance" was answered with: "Three weeks is not a right balance of anything… customers are going to start blaming you (even legally)."

Defence & nuance

  • Most complainers don't pay: "freeloaders get freeloader service" (although paying customers didn't get the fix either).
  • RHEL was also about two weeks late. "This is poor form from several vendors that tout 'we'll handle XYZ aspect of security for you'."
  • The fix existed in -proposed, so self-serve users could install it.
  • "Enterprise customers just call their account managers rather than write a blog."
"The whole point of paying for Ubuntu enterprise services is you don't have to worry about all this nonsense."sick_of_slop
Update, Nov 12: Standard LTS kernels shipped on Nov 10 (26 days). Pro-only ESM releases (20.04, 18.04, 16.04) and FIPS kernels are still unfixed, so paying customers are now behind free users.
Implication: The paid tier didn't buy faster delivery, so the value of Pro is now openly questioned. Publish a CVE SLA by severity tier (e.g. critical RCE → expedited SRU in N days) and give Pro an expedited channel that clearly beats the free tier.

Data security

Epicentre

Comprehensive data protection, encryption at rest, and secure backups for business continuity.

Criticism

  • A remote, pre-auth kernel use-after-free in the TCP stack could be a "total takeover". Users took servers offline (the OP, and a commenter who shut down a personal site and tools).
  • Ubuntu's advisory listed no mitigation, while Red Hat and AWS published one-line firewall rules (iptables … u32 '0>>24&0x0f=6:15' -j DROP / nft ip hdrlength gt 5).
  • Correcting a common misreading: MPTCP isn't required. Any TCP packet carrying IP options can trigger the bug. "sysctl net.mptcp.enabled=0" was called "misinformation".
  • Livepatch didn't deliver either: "So their live patching is a larp too."
  • PwnEscape (KVM breakout) and the earlier brotli and PHP CVEs were cited as a pattern of slow fixes.

Defence & nuance

  • EPSS was under 1%. Most advisories describe a crash or DoS, and only Anthropic claims RCE ("trust me bro").
  • Most internet paths and cloud LBs (AWS, GCP) already drop IP options. KASLR makes reliable root hard.
  • No mass compromises had been observed. A commenter said the "9.8 is probably too high".
  • Counterpoint: "As the days pass the probability of it being actually used is increasing." Untrusted LANs and public Wi-Fi remain exposed.
"I would be perfectly ok if they had either quietly patched it earlier (like Debian), or communicated their prioritization, or … provided a high quality advisory (like Red Hat)."BoppreH
Update, Nov 12: The CVE page now carries a detailed nftables mitigation (added around Nov 11), but the Nov 9 blog still says "security updates are the only appropriate mitigation". No Livepatch notice has been issued.
Implication: The gap wasn't only about patch speed. Canonical gave customers no interim protection. Ship mitigations on day 0 (firewall rules, Livepatch where feasible, Landscape-pushable configs) whenever a kernel SRU can't go out immediately.

Percorr: performance & correctness

Negative

Consistent, high-performance execution, and confidence in what's happening under the hood and up the dependency chain.

Criticism ("black box")

  • The CVE page was opaque. "Why this priority?" just said "See ubuntu cvss score" with no full stop. The description was copied from NIST, and the affected-package list didn't name linux-image-* ("feels like this advisory wasn't written or even reviewed by a human").
  • The headline showed CVSS 3 at 9.8, but the priority was set using CVSS 4 at 8.3 (impact Low/Low/High). Nobody could work out the rationale.
  • The page still marked kernels as "Vulnerable" after fixes were in -proposed or -updates, and the changelogs didn't mention the CVE.
  • The regressions from the fixed kernel (AppArmor breaking k8s ingress and Postgres signals) undermined the claim that two weeks of testing guarantees quality.
  • Previous breakages: ZFS data-loss fix took about 6 months, upgrades renamed NICs and dropped routes, kernel patches broke containers, plus iptables and nilfs problems.

Defence & strengths

  • "Their kernel is vastly superior when dealing with buggy enterprise hardware." Examples: Broadcom 10GbE dropouts and E810/disk stalls that don't happen on Ubuntu.
  • HWE kernels, ZFS without DKMS, and major-version upgrades that are "a lot more reliable" than on Debian.
  • Canonical maintains "dozens of kernels" across chipsets. Rushing a backport risks bricking fleets, and certification testing exists for good reason.
  • "Canonical's security record has been exemplary in my experience."
"I think the really messed up thing is the lack of communication… the lack of transparency is causing a loss of confidence in the team's abilities."mey
Update, Nov 12: The priority was raised from High to critical, and the CVE page now matches what has shipped. But the "Priority reason" still just reads "See ubuntu cvss score".
Implication: Confidence depends on explained decisions. Every High/Critical CVE needs a human-written rationale, a mitigation, and a live SRU status (build → -proposed → certification → -security) with ETAs.

Engineering efficiency & cost

Negative

Faster development, freeing engineers to tackle core problems, and avoiding lock-in.

Criticism

  • Lots of unplanned toil: mitigations, then "now I'm waiting for patch to unwind my mitigations", manual pinning of -proposed, mainline kernels, borrowing the Proxmox kernel, or LLM-built livepatches.
  • "Ubuntu Pro is $500 per machine" yet brought no benefit here. "Paid support. Doesn't look like it's worth it, though."
  • Lock-in friction: snaps (browsers forced, they break in Docker, you have to mirror both repos on air-gapped networks), Pro upsells in motd and apt ("basically Windows"), and Rust coreutils pushed as the default.
  • Migration is getting cheap. "Cost is hours and a dollar in tokens" with agents, and someone moved to Rocky in about 4 hours.

Why people stay (switching costs)

  • Inertia: it's the default image on AWS, DigitalOcean, Azure, Oracle, WSL, and Raspberry Pi.
  • Vendors build for Ubuntu first: CUDA/DGX OS, Jetson, ROS, AMD ROCm/Vivado, SQL Server, llama.cpp, Steam.
  • Desktop polish, drivers, and a low-RTFM community. "Adding 'on ubuntu' to a search gives me better tutorials."
  • A clear free-to-paid path: "Ubuntu makes it a lot easier than Red Hat."
"Taking them down and putting them up would take a lot of time and risk… So, I find myself taking the security gamble instead."lynndotpy
Implication: Retention currently relies on ecosystem gravity and switching costs, not on perceived value. Upsell nags combined with a missed security promise is the worst mix. Show concrete Pro outcomes, such as faster or exclusive fixes and Livepatch coverage.

Compliance & certifications

Moat, under pressure

Software that meets industry standards and regulations, to mitigate legal risk and keep stakeholders' trust.

Where Ubuntu still wins

  • A predictable LTS ("5 years… option to pay for another 5 ticks the box for accreditors") versus Debian's "vaguer" volunteer LTS and ELTS.
  • FIPS-validated modules and kernel off the shelf. Debian has openssl-provider-fips, but it isn't a validated build, so you'd need a third party like WolfSSL.
  • Supported-platform lists: SQL Server (Windows/Ubuntu/RHEL) and NVIDIA DGX OS. "It's not worth the effort dealing with management/auditors."
  • "Enterprise support contracts… and compliance legal liability. No large commercial companies would run purely contributor supported systems."

Where it cracked

  • Canonical cited Ubuntu Certified hardware testing as the reason for the delay, so certification became the bottleneck rather than the value.
  • "A secure software without support is worse from an audit perspective than a security disaster with proper paperwork": a cynical read that the value is only paperwork.
  • Critical-CVE timelines in SOC2/FedRAMP-style regimes can't be met with an 18+ day gap. Liability risk was raised.
  • For regulated shops, the alternative mentioned was RHEL-family (Rocky, Alma), not Debian.
"It isn't just compiling something that works… What you are paying for is… reducing the friction when you go through an audit."thayne
Update, Nov 12: linux-fips is still pending on 22.04 and 24.04, and needed on 20.04, 18.04 and 16.04, including the AWS, Azure and GCP FIPS kernels. The customers who stay for compliance are the most exposed. RHEL's FIPS-capable kernels shipped Nov 1.
Implication: Compliance remains the strongest reason to stay. Protect it by making the certification pipeline able to fast-track critical CVEs, so it isn't seen as the cause of delay.

Scale

Mixed

Designed for growing workloads, user bases, and data volumes, and also for operating at scale.

Criticism

  • Fleet operators can't patch at scale if the vendor pocket is empty. The options are risky -proposed pinning across every node, or taking servers offline.
  • A kernel regression hit a Kubernetes node and cascaded across 7+ apps: "Kubernetes node went to pieces."
  • "I'm moving all my servers off Ubuntu." — "I wish life was that easy." Non-IaC fleets feel stuck.
  • Lagging rollouts cause big-org process drag: "techno-bureaucrats… dogmatically sticking with their release cadence".

Defence & strengths

  • Huge install base and defaults across clouds. Users run Ubuntu everywhere (Pis, WSL, servers) "for consistency".
  • 10–15-year support for many LTS and HWE kernel lines is real operational value, at the cost of a large testing matrix.
  • Phased updates and remote OS upgrades work well. One commenter: "low probability that applying updates will cause some catastrophic outage… over the next 10 years".
  • NixOS users pointed to declarative rollouts and SBOMs as the bar for patching fleets.
"Unlike Arch or even Debian, Canonical has dozens of kernels to test and verify… Testing all of those takes time."jeroenhd
Implication: Breadth of support is both the selling point and the cause of slowness. Pair it with fleet tooling (Landscape) that can push mitigations and staged -proposed kernels safely.

APIs & analytics

Weak signal

Extensible integration with existing tools and BI, custom workflows, and actionable insights.

Observations

  • Security data didn't match reality. The CVE tracker said "Vulnerable" while kernels were already shipped, which breaks scanners and dashboards that rely on it.
  • Package naming was inconsistent (linux vs linux-image-* / linux-signed-hwe-*), so machine-readable status was hard to map to installed packages.
  • People had to dig through Launchpad SRU workflow bugs to find the real state ("passed everything but testing").
  • Workaround: users set up AI agents to poll the Ubuntu website daily for the kernel update.

Opportunity

  • An accurate, real-time CVE/OVAL/VEX feed with SRU stage and ETA would remove most of the confusion.
  • NixOS's SBOM and dependency-tracking quality was praised as the standard to match.
Implication: Treat CVE status as an API product. Its accuracy directly drives customer tooling and audit evidence.

Audit logs

Indirect

Tracking user actions, system changes, and access attempts for accountability and security investigations.

Observations

  • Changelog traceability: the CVE wasn't listed in the changelogs of the new kernels (possibly because of the embargo). An auditor can't prove remediation from those packages alone.
  • Without a timestamped status history on the CVE page, nobody can tell when a fix actually landed (Oct 28 vs "still vulnerable").
  • Teams need evidence for the people who decide on downtime: "they're not particularly interested in the details… just if it's critical and has an exploit".
  • Nov 12: the fix shipped inside 15 USNs, most of them bundling 1,100–3,500 CVEs ("+3273 others"). Only the NVIDIA BaseOS notice is narrow. There's no single advisory to point an auditor at.

Opportunity

  • Give each CVE an audit-ready trail: timestamps per pocket, linked commits, and the mitigation-applied state reported via Landscape.
Implication: Remediation evidence is part of the compliance product. Make it exportable.

Authorization + IAM

Indirect

Fine-grained access control for complex orgs and data sensitivity levels, plus SSO and MFA.

Observations

  • The CVE is about losing authorization entirely: root via an unauthenticated packet. Every IAM control above the kernel is moot until it's patched.
  • Users are giving AI agents root or SSH to self-patch ("agents with root access… crossed over the threshold of being more secure"), while others refuse ("not trusting LLM… my critical infrastructure"). This is an emerging governance gap.
  • Secure Boot signed-kernel requirements stop users from building their own fixed kernel. Adding keys needs a pre-boot client action ("What an awful system").

Opportunity

  • Role-scoped, auditable remediation automation (for example Landscape with approvals) as the safe alternative to an "agent with root".
Implication: When vendors are slow, customers grant wide privileges to ad-hoc tools. Fast patching is itself an access-control measure.

Outside the pillars: brand & community trust

Long-tail resentment

These themes don't fit an enterprise pillar, but they amplified the backlash and steer how practitioners recommend distros.

  • Accumulated grievances: the Amazon "spyware" lens, ads in motd, Pro nags in apt, snaps, Rust uutils, the forced Wayland move, killed side projects.
  • "Ubuntu is a great starter linux. But you cant live on it, especially in a business."
  • The influencer pipeline is at risk: "People like me will simply post… that they should not install Ubuntu… Cut the pipeline that feeds into it." A consultant changed their advice from "not recommended" to "switch immediately".
  • Several jokes about the hiring process (SAT and high-school scores) used as a stand-in for the company's culture.
  • Counter-voices: "Calm down… Ubuntu is doing o.k. here", "Despite the recent hate, Ubuntu is still serving me very well", and pushback against "I demand to speak to the manager" energy in OSS.
  • Some suspected the hype was AI or Anthropic marketing.
